What happens when you inherit a house in Missouri?

Published April 18, 2026 8 min read By the Restore STL Team

The phone call comes, and then a week later the mail starts arriving. A letter from the county. A statement from a mortgage servicer you've never heard of. An insurance renewal. Somewhere in the pile is a house — your parents' house, or your aunt's, or a grandparent's — and now it's partly yours.

Most people we talk to don't know what to do first. That's not a knowledge gap to feel bad about: Missouri probate moves on its own clock, and the rules genuinely change depending on whether there was a will, how the deed was titled, and whether anyone else inherited alongside you. This piece is the plain-English version of what actually happens, what you control, and what can wait.

The first 30 days: what probate actually means

Probate is just the legal process of transferring what someone owned into the names of the people they left it to. In Missouri, that usually runs through the circuit court in the county where the person lived — St. Louis City, St. Louis County, St. Charles, Jefferson, wherever the house is. If the estate is small enough or the deed was titled a certain way (joint tenancy with right of survivorship, or a beneficiary deed), you may skip full probate entirely. If not, expect the process to take six to twelve months minimum.

During that window, the house isn't quite yours yet — not in a way that lets you sell it on the open market without the court's sign-off. But you can, and should, do three things right away: secure the property, keep the insurance active, and gather the paperwork (deed, mortgage statement, most recent tax bill, any known debts). That's it for week one.

If multiple people inherited, the executor (or "personal representative" in Missouri's language) is the one with authority to act. Everyone else has standing to be informed and to object — but not to make unilateral decisions. Sort out who that person is early. It prevents the most common family friction we see.

Your three real options

Once the legal side is moving, the question becomes what to actually do with the house. There are only three real answers, and they're worth naming plainly:

Keep it and live in it. Straightforward if one heir wants the house and can buy out the others at a fair appraisal. Harder when two heirs both want it, or when the person who wants it can't afford to buy the other siblings out.

Keep it and rent it. Makes sense if the house is in good shape, the numbers pencil as a rental, and at least one co-heir wants to manage it (or pay for management). Worth the math, not worth the assumption. STL rental yields vary wildly by ZIP.

Sell it. Either on the open market with an agent, or directly to a buyer who takes it as-is. Which one is right depends almost entirely on condition, timeline, and how much friction the family can absorb.

What St. Louis families most often get wrong

The biggest mistake isn't financial — it's procedural. Families rush to list the house in the first month because it feels like an action, only to find out six weeks later that the court hasn't authorized the sale yet and the listing has to be pulled. Then the Zillow history shows a stale listing, which hurts the eventual sale price.

The second-biggest mistake is doing repairs before anyone has decided what's happening. New carpet in an inherited house that's going to be sold as-is is money you'll never get back. New carpet in one that's being kept for a grandkid to live in is reasonable. The order matters: decide first, spend second.

The third mistake is emotional, and harder to avoid. The house often represents decades of a person's life. Every decision feels bigger than it is. Give yourself permission to move slowly on the parts that matter (sorting belongings, keeping a few things) and quickly on the parts that don't (deciding whether to keep the utilities on for another month).

Most families don't need to sell the house in week one. They need to know what the house is actually worth, and what their options really are. That alone takes the pressure off.

What to do this week

If you're reading this in the first month after a death, here's a short list of things that are worth doing now. None of them commit you to a direction.

  • Keep the house insuredLapsed coverage is the single most expensive mistake. Call the current carrier; most will extend a vacant-property rider.
  • Find the deed and the mortgage statementYou'll need both for every next step. Check filing cabinets, safe-deposit boxes, and email.
  • Identify the executor, in writingEven before probate opens. Whoever is named in the will, or the closest next of kin, should be the decision-maker.
  • Get a real number on the house's valueNot a Zillow estimate — an actual comp-based opinion from someone who knows the neighborhood. We'll do this for free; so will most agents.

None of this requires you to make a final decision. The point of this week is to give yourself information, so that when the probate process catches up, you already know what you want to do.

The families who handle this well aren't the ones who move fastest. They're the ones who get clear on the numbers early, agree on who's deciding, and then give themselves the time to pick the right option — not the first one.
